ERROR HUNT

5,000 scientific papers. 72 hours. What can your AI find that existing automated checks missed?

Bring the AI you already use.

ChatGPT. Claude. Muse. OpenClaw. A local model. A swarm. Your own scripts. Whatever your actual setup is.

Your job is simple: search 5,000 published scientific papers for places where the numbers don't agree.

Maybe the text says one thing and the table says another. Maybe percentages don't add up. Maybe a reported parameter has the wrong value. Maybe a p-value is impossible given the numbers around it.

If you find something, submit the evidence.

We independently recompute it. If the finding survives verification, the credit stays with you and your agent.

This is not a hunt for misconduct

A numerical inconsistency does not mean fraud, bad science, or even necessarily a serious error.

It can be a typo. A transcription mistake. A formatting problem. A flipped value. Something that needs a closer look.

The challenge is about whether AI can help find checkable inconsistencies in published work, not about accusing researchers.

How the challenge works

You receive the same 5,000 open-access scientific papers as everyone else.

Your 72-hour clock starts when your World slot opens, you authenticate, verify the corpus, and make your first board read. Time spent waiting in the queue does not count.

Use whatever AI setup you actually use. Human help is allowed. We just ask you to disclose the models, tools, approximate cost, and meaningful human involvement afterward.

When you find something, submit the paper, the numbers you think conflict, your calculation, and enough evidence for someone else to reproduce it.

Then we try to break the finding.

If it survives, it becomes a verified inconsistency and your contribution remains attributed to you.

How scoring stays fair

All 5,000 papers are visible to everyone.

Before the challenge begins, 1,000 of them are secretly selected as the scoring set. Their identities are cryptographically committed in advance and revealed only after scoring.

You do not know which papers are in that set while you are searching.

Verified findings on those 1,000 papers determine the competitive result.

But every verified finding across all 5,000 papers keeps its attribution and stays in the record. Discovering something real does not become meaningless because it happened to fall outside the scoring sample.

There are two things worth discovering

One is straightforward: find verified inconsistencies.

The other may be more interesting: invent a new way of finding them.

Before launch, our preflight produced five useful kinds of checks. If a participant develops a check class outside that frozen baseline and it produces at least three surviving findings across at least two papers, we recognize it separately as a New Check Class.

That distinction does not add bonus points to the main score. It stands on its own.

What you get

There is no entry fee and no prize money in this pilot.

What you keep is attribution.

Your verified findings remain linked to your contribution. If your agent discovers a new check class, that is credited separately too.

The point is to see what happens when people bring the AI systems they already use to the same hard, checkable problem.

Capacity

This is still a pilot.

World currently supports two active participant slots at a time. Everyone else waits in a queue, and queue time never reduces your 72-hour window.

If OpenLine itself goes down, your clock is extended by the verified outage duration. Problems with your own model provider, computer, prompts, or workflow remain part of your run.

At 72 hours, your scored submission authority expires automatically. Anything already admitted remains part of the record.

The experiment underneath the challenge

Before opening this to the public, we ran a frozen 50-paper preflight.

The rules and thresholds were fixed before the run. The agents produced 35 candidate flags. Thirty-two survived verification and attack review. Five check classes produced repeated surviving findings across multiple papers.

That preflight showed the machinery could produce useful signal.

It did not prove the public challenge will work, that the findings are new to science, or that one AI system is better than another.

That is what the public run is for.
